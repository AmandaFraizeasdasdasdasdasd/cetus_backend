Ocean drilling & seismic survey exposure

How exploration activity reaches whales

Oil and gas exploration locates seabed reserves with seismic airgun arrays — towed devices that fire a compressed-air blast every 10–15 seconds for the duration of a survey, sometimes weeks. The resulting low-frequency noise travels far more efficiently underwater than in air.

  • Behavioral range: controlled studies on humpbacks (the BRAHSS project) found airgun noise altered swimming and vocal behavior at distances up to ~3 km from the source.
  • Documented harms: hearing damage, disrupted communication and feeding, displacement from surveyed habitat, and — in some species such as narwhals, which freeze rather than flee — elevated stranding risk.
  • Beyond noise: platform and rig placement itself fragments habitat, support-vessel traffic raises ship-strike risk, and an active well always carries some spill risk to feeding and calving grounds.

Why this belongs on a tracking dashboard

Overlaying proposed or active survey/lease blocks against real occurrence data is how this becomes decision-useful rather than just descriptive — it shows whether a corridor a client cares about actually overlaps operational activity, and when.

The map now overlays real active Gulf of Mexico oil & gas lease boundaries from BOEM — toggle it on to see actual lease areas rather than an illustrative example. Coverage is still Gulf of Mexico only, not global.

Whaling pressure & anti-whaling campaigns

Commercial whaling & the moratorium

Industrial whaling drove several great whale populations — including North Atlantic humpbacks — to the edge of collapse through the mid-20th century. The International Whaling Commission's 1986 moratorium halted most commercial whaling, and it's the main reason population-recovery trends like the one charted above exist at all.

  • The loophole: Japan continued Antarctic whaling for decades under a "scientific research" permit, a workaround the moratorium allowed but conservation groups argued was commercial whaling in disguise.
  • Direct-action response: Sea Shepherd Conservation Society ran annual campaigns shadowing and blocking Japan's whaling fleet in the Southern Ocean, including its factory ship, the Nisshin Maru.

Notable campaign history

Sea Shepherd's fleet became well known through these campaigns, funded in part by high-profile donors — Bob Barker's $5 million gift funded the ship named in his honor, the MY Bob Barker, which spent years actively interfering with the Antarctic whaling fleet.

  • Tactics included physically blocking harpoon vessels, fouling propellers, and shadowing the fleet to prevent hunts.
  • Confrontations sometimes escalated — the Sea Shepherd vessel Ady Gil was lost in a 2010 collision with a Japanese security vessel.
  • Japan formally withdrew from the IWC in 2019 and resumed commercial whaling within its own territorial waters, ending the Antarctic "research" hunts Sea Shepherd had targeted.

Whales in captivity — current status~54 orcas worldwide

Where things stand

Captivity is almost entirely an orca story — few other whale species are held long-term. As of early 2026, an estimated 53–55 orcas remain in captivity worldwide, down from a peak of well over a hundred.

  • SeaWorld holds around 16 orcas across its San Diego, Orlando, and San Antonio parks — the largest single holder, and the last in the Western Hemisphere.
  • SeaWorld ended its captive breeding program in 2016, following sustained public pressure after the documentary Blackfish, and hasn't captured a wild orca since the 1980s.
  • Wild capture hasn't fully stopped globally — a 2018 capture of 10 orcas in Russian waters, destined for Chinese parks, drew international condemnation before most were released back to the wild in 2019.

Where it's heading

The direction is toward retirement rather than replacement: no major Western facility is currently breeding or acquiring new orcas, and momentum has shifted toward seaside sanctuaries — netted-off coastal coves — as a more natural retirement option than a concrete tank, though none currently house a former SeaWorld orca.

  • Captive population counts — Whale & Dolphin Conservation, Wikipedia (List of captive orcas)
  • SeaWorld history & breeding ban — International Marine Mammal Project / Earth Island Institute

SeaWorld's actual international footprint

SeaWorld's parent company (now United Parks & Resorts) has stayed almost entirely domestic. Its only international venture is SeaWorld Abu Dhabi, a brand-new, ground-up park that opened in 2023 — not an acquisition of an existing or struggling facility.

  • SeaWorld doesn't own or operate the Abu Dhabi park — it's owned and run by Miral, an Emirati developer, with SeaWorld licensing its brand, animal-care protocols, and theme design.
  • SeaWorld Abu Dhabi does not hold orcas.
  • The "buying up struggling foreign parks" pattern sometimes attributed to SeaWorld actually belongs to a different company: Six Flags, which bought a failing Mexico City park (Reino Aventura — once home to Keiko, the orca from Free Willy) in 1999 and rebranded it as Six Flags México.

A related ownership thread worth knowing

Before Blackstone (SeaWorld's owner when Blackfish released in 2013), SeaWorld belonged to Anheuser-Busch's theme-park division. When Belgian-Brazilian brewer InBev acquired Anheuser-Busch in 2008, it had no interest in theme parks and spun off SeaWorld/Busch Gardens to Blackstone shortly after — a corporate reshuffle, not an international expansion.

How to help — where to start

See a whale in distress? Report it — don't act

The single most useful thing an individual can do is report, not intervene. Only NOAA-authorized responders are trained and equipped to safely approach an entangled or injured whale — getting close yourself, even to help, can hurt both of you.

  • Call the regional 24-hour hotline for your area: West Coast (CA/OR/WA) (877) 767-9425 · Southeast/Gulf/Caribbean (877) 942-5343 · Alaska (877) 925-7773 · Greater Atlantic (VA–Canada border) (866) 755-6622
  • Or hail the US Coast Guard on VHF Channel 16 if you're on the water and don't have a number handy
  • Or use the free "Dolphin and Whale 911" app (NOAA, iOS and Android) to report from anywhere
  • Keep a safe, legal distance and stay with the animal from afar until responders arrive if you safely can — that's the most helpful thing a bystander can do

Ongoing ways to help

  • Submit your own whale photos to Happywhale.com — real citizen-science fluke-ID photos feed directly into the kind of research data this dashboard uses
  • Follow whale-watching distance guidelines — NOAA recommends staying at least 100 yards from whales generally (greater distances apply for some protected species); slow down in areas whales are known to frequent
  • Choose sustainably-caught seafood — gear entanglement is one of the leading causes of whale injury and death; sustainable fisheries use whale-safe gear
  • Reduce plastic and ocean pollution at the source — much of it ends up as the marine debris whales become entangled in
  • Support organizations working directly on this: NOAA Fisheries, Whale and Dolphin Conservation (WDC), International Fund for Animal Welfare (IFAW), Ocean Conservancy
